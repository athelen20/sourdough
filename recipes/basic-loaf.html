Basic sourdough loaf

One round loaf, baked in a covered pot. Mixed in the evening, shaped at night, baked the next morning. Nothing clever, and it works.

Before you start

Your starter must be properly established, doubling reliably within 4–8 hours. A newly revived starter on day five will not raise a loaf, and the result will be a dense brick that makes you think you did something wrong. Give it a full week or two of good feeds first.

Ingredients

IngredientWeightBaker's %
Bread flour500 g100%
Water, lukewarm350 g70%
Active starter, at peak100 g20%
Fine salt10 g2%

King Arthur bread flour is what Snow is used to. If you only have all-purpose flour, drop the water to about 325 g, as it absorbs less.

Equipment

  • A large mixing bowl and a digital scale
  • A cast iron pot or any oven-safe pot with a lid, around 4–5 litres
  • A banneton, or a bowl lined with a well-floured tea towel
  • A sharp knife, razor blade or lame for scoring
  • Baking parchment

Method

Step 1 · feed the starter

The morning of the day you want to mix, feed your starter so it will be at its peak that evening. A 1:2:2 feed (for example 20 g starter, 40 g water, 40 g flour) typically peaks in 6 to 8 hours.

Step 2 · autolyse, 30–60 min

Mix the 500 g flour and 350 g water until no dry flour remains. It will look shaggy and unpleasant. Cover and rest for 30 to 60 minutes.

This step does real work: the flour hydrates and gluten starts forming on its own, so the dough becomes far easier to handle.

Step 3 · add starter and salt

Add 100 g active starter and 10 g salt. Squeeze and fold the dough with wet hands for a couple of minutes until both are fully worked through. The dough will feel slack and sticky. That is correct.

Step 4 · bulk ferment, 4–6 hours

Cover the bowl and leave it somewhere warm. During the first two hours, do a set of stretch and folds every 30 minutes — four sets in total.

To do a set: with a wet hand, grab one side of the dough, stretch it upwards until it resists, and fold it over the middle. Turn the bowl a quarter and repeat, four times in all. Cover and leave.

After the folds, leave it undisturbed for the rest of the bulk ferment. It is ready when it has grown by roughly 50 %, looks domed and jiggly, and has visible bubbles at the surface and edges.

Judge by look, not by clock. In a warm kitchen this can be 3 hours; in a cold one, 8. Under-fermented dough gives a dense loaf; over-fermented dough spreads flat and tears. Aim for about 50% growth and you will be close.

Step 5 · shape

Tip the dough onto a lightly floured counter. Fold the edges into the middle to form a rough ball, flip it seam-side down, then drag it towards you a few times with cupped hands so the surface tightens into a smooth, taut skin.

Place it seam side up into a floured banneton or towel-lined bowl.

Step 6 · final proof, overnight

Cover and put it in the fridge for 8 to 16 hours. This deepens the flavour, firms up the dough so it is far easier to score, and lets you bake whenever suits you the next day.

In a hurry? Proof at room temperature for 1 to 2 hours instead, until a floured finger pressed into the dough leaves a dent that springs back slowly.

Step 7 · bake

Put the pot with its lid on into the oven and heat to 250 °C. Let it heat for a full 45 minutes so the pot is genuinely hot.

Tip the cold dough out onto a square of baking parchment. Score the top with one confident cut about 1 cm deep, slightly off-centre and at a shallow angle.

Lift the dough in by the parchment, lid on, and bake:

  • 20 minutes at 250 °C with the lid on
  • then remove the lid, drop to 220 °C and bake a further 20–25 minutes until deeply browned

The trapped steam under the lid is what gives you a good rise and a crisp crust. It is the reason for the pot.

Step 8 · cool, and wait

Cool on a wire rack for at least an hour, ideally two, before cutting. The loaf is still cooking inside as it cools, and slicing it hot gives you a gummy crumb. This is the hardest step.

If the first one is bad

It very often is, and it is usually the starter rather than the baker. Dense and gummy means it needed more strength or more time. Feed for another week and try again. Nearly everyone's third loaf is dramatically better than their first.
