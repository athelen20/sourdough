Glossary

Sourdough people use a lot of jargon. None of it is complicated once translated.

Starter

The jar of flour and water you keep alive and feed. Also called a culture, a mother, or a chef.

Levain

A portion of starter built up specially for one bake, often with different flour or a different ratio. In practice, for home baking, you can treat "levain" and "active starter" as the same thing.

Discard

The starter you remove before feeding. Not rubbish — it is unleavened but perfectly edible, and makes excellent pancakes and crackers.

Hooch

The dark liquid that collects on a hungry starter. Alcohol produced by the yeast. Pour it off or stir it in, then feed.

Hydration

Water as a percentage of flour weight. Equal weights of each is 100% hydration, which is the standard for a starter. Bread doughs are usually 65–80%.

Baker's percentage

A way of writing recipes where flour is always 100% and everything else is expressed relative to it. 70% water with 500 g flour means 350 g water. It makes scaling a recipe up or down trivial.

Autolyse

Mixing just flour and water and letting it rest before adding starter and salt. It lets the flour hydrate fully and makes the dough easier to handle.

Bulk ferment

The first long rise, with all the dough in one mass, after mixing and before shaping. This is where most of the flavour and structure develops.

Stretch and fold

Instead of kneading, you lift one side of the dough, stretch it up and fold it over, then rotate and repeat. Done a few times during the bulk ferment, it builds strength gently.

Shaping

Forming the dough into a tight ball or oval so it has surface tension and holds its shape in the oven rather than spreading out.

Banneton

A basket, usually cane, that supports the shaped dough during its final rise. A bowl lined with a floured tea towel does exactly the same job.

Proof / final proof

The last rise after shaping, before baking.

Retard

Slowing fermentation down by putting the dough in the fridge, usually overnight. It deepens flavour and makes the timing fit around real life.

Score

The cut you slash into the top of the loaf just before baking, so it expands in a controlled direction instead of bursting at a random seam.

Oven spring

The dramatic final rise in the first ten minutes of baking, as the gas in the dough expands in the heat.

Crumb

The inside of the loaf. An open crumb has large irregular holes, a tight crumb is fine and even. Neither is better, they just suit different purposes.

Peak

The moment your starter has risen as far as it will go and is about to start sinking. This is when it is strongest and when you should use it.
